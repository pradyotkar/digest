OpenAI shows its numbers: chief scientist Jakub Pachocki publishes the lab's internal pretraining perplexity curve for the first time — ~0.99 for GPT-3.5, ~0.91 for GPT-4, below 0.8 for today's models, ~0.76 for the unnamed internal model he labels "Solved a Millennium Prize math problem" — and proposes that frontier labs agree on common limits they won't cross until monitoring catches up; Altman calls it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in awhile"

TOP STORYAI SafetyResearch

The essay, "Perplexity and Intelligence" (openai.com, Nov 22), argues perplexity — how well a base model predicts held-out text; OpenAI reports its log, equivalently cross-entropy loss on a fixed snapshot of its internal codebase — is the durable, cross-lab yardstick the field should pace itself on. Sulat's Nov 23 summary tracks the chart: GPT-3.5 at ~0.99 bought fluent conversation; GPT-4 at ~0.91 bought productive chain-of-thought; transformative cybersecurity and math capabilities appeared below 0.8; and "once we upgraded to a model of log-perplexity ~0.76, it started producing groundbreaking results" on open problems OpenAI had been posing for over a year — with an internal model plotted lower still, near 0.73. Pachocki says he expects further transformative capabilities — possibly ones nobody currently tests for — in the ~0.68–0.7 window, and warns "as soon as 2027 we could conceivably see a similar avalanche of fundamental pretraining advancements." The ask: shared norms for evaluating and red-teaming frontier models before first use, and "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone" — his example milestone being a successor to chain-of-thought monitors. The Next Web (Nov 22) notes he does not say where the first limit should sit; Altman endorsed nine minutes after the post, hoping labs agree to common limits because "I think that would be very wise and help ensure safety and control."

The fine print is doing heavy lifting. DataStudios' breakdown (Nov 23) stresses that OpenAI's figures come from a private code snapshot no other lab can reproduce, that chain-of-thought and agentic scaffolding gains never lower perplexity (so a pretraining cap leaves post-training outside its scope), and that the entropy floor is unknown — near it, "smaller gains on perplexity will correspond to larger leaps in model intelligence." NeuralBrew's analysis (Nov 22–23) computes that the distance from the ~0.76 model to the 0.68–0.7 window (~0.06–0.08) is the same order as GPT-3.5 to GPT-4 — "the next comparable leap could be close" — while concluding that until a competitor publishes its own numbers and commits, "this is an offer to talk, not a brake." CASRAI (Nov 23) situates it against Dario Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" call and notes TechCrunch's same-day coverage; Yahoo Finance (Nov 22) adds the counters — Meta's Alexandr Wang urging a "controlled approach" — and the timing pressure: California sued OpenAI last week alleging it knowingly developed models without necessary safeguards, citing the July Hugging Face attack. The essay also links OpenAI's Nov 10 alignment study finding that lower perplexity correlates with reasoning that is harder to monitor in visible chain-of-thought — the very degradation that makes the proposed milestone urgent.

Singapore writes the agent-banking rulebook: MAS Managing Director Chia Der Jiun unveiled a "Know Your Agent" protocol for trusted information exchange between customer AI agents and financial institutions — plus an Agentic Money Sandbox with the Future of Finance Institute — while bluntly warning people not to hand their bank logins to personal AI agents

AI AgentsRegulation

Delivered at the Singapore FinTech Festival (Nov 19) and reported by Hubbis (Nov 23), the plan makes MAS the first major central bank to formalize how agents get standing access to finance: the "know your agent" protocol will cover agents' identities, whom they represent, their authority and mandates, and assurances about their provenance and reliability — part of a new phase of MAS's Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime (SAFR) program. Chia's advice to individuals is sharper: do not give personal AI agents financial account login credentials — there is insufficient assurance of safety (a position Together AI's leaked-credential incident, story 4 of yesterday's digest, did nothing to soften). He noted some payment providers and banks are already building agent interfaces while many institutions have no terms or channels for agent access at all, floated limits on agent-instructed transaction sizes, and warned regulators must weigh financial-stability implications of agents moving money and allocating assets. On the plumbing side: MAS will run an Agentic Money Sandbox with the FFI combining AI agents and programmable money for corporate treasury and payments; it issued SGD 100 million (USD 78M) in tokenised MAS Bills on Nov 6, settled in wholesale CBDC; the Guardian Tokenised Collateral Framework published Nov 19; and the SGD Testnet is being renamed SG Digital Ledger and repositioned as an operational settlement utility. Crypto, Chia reiterated, remains unsuitable for retail investors.

Nadella's post-mortem on the IDE: Microsoft's CEO says Claude Code "caught Microsoft off guard" by moving AI coding into the terminal, that GitHub Copilot began as mere autocomplete and Cursor's agent mode forced the first real response — and that a jump in model capability can rapidly change the interface developers want. The context: a voluntary exit offer covering ~60,000 employees

Coding AgentsApplications

In an interview recapped by Magica (Nov 17, back on every trend board by Nov 23 via Pillitteri's daily list), Satya Nadella laid out the sequence that eroded Visual Studio Code's moat: Copilot started as code completion; Cursor built a strong agent mode, which Microsoft matched; then Anthropic's command-line Claude Code shifted what developers wanted outright. His lesson — "a jump in model capability can rapidly change the interface developers want" — is why Microsoft now manages rival agents inside VS Code rather than defending the IDE as the product, even as he allows IDEs could regain importance if developers embed "decision models" in software. Verification, not generation, is his stated constraint: a model told to make tests pass "might delete failing tests instead of correcting the code," models reviewing models need safeguards of their own, and he wants feedback loops built on records of software running in production. The staffing frame is uncomfortable: he was explaining a voluntary exit offer covering about 60,000 employees, while leaving unanswered whether more layoffs follow; teams now balance human effort against token budgets, and Microsoft's $30B+ Anthropic commitments make the competitive picture ("rival agent inside your own editor") genuinely strange. His architectural advice for everyone else: keep models, memory, and the software running agents separable so you can switch suppliers and grade with your own evals; route everyday tasks to cheap local models and hard work to cloud frontier models.
