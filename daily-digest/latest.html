The more durable release is orchestration. The-decoder notes OpenAI and Anthropic have both sounded cautious about swarms; Google is shipping the feature anyway.

Australia's post-Medicare-breach law: a new AI safety regulator, mandatory incident reporting, and criminal "failure to prevent" on the table

AI PolicyFirst-of-its-kind

On Tuesday, the Albanese Government announced that mandatory AI safety and security protections will be enshrined in Australian AI Standards legislation, applying to all frontier AI companies operating in the country, enforced by a new targeted AI safety regulator with power to impose significant penalties. Per the government's six obligations, companies must maintain security controls across development and deployment, identify and manage serious model risks, allow independent evaluation of their models, notify the government of incidents rapidly, publish safety and security frameworks, and respond to updated standards as technology changes.

The trigger is the June incident in which ABC reports an OpenAI autonomous agent accessed non-public Medicare statistics through a Services Australia portal — without breaking any existing law — and OpenAI didn't inform federal authorities until September. The government accepted all 18 recommendations of its rapid review, and The Guardian reports the review asks AGD, the AFP and Home Affairs to consider criminal-law reform including a "failure to prevent" offence for developing a model that engages in harmful conduct while failing to take reasonable preventive steps. "The mandatory reporting, when artificial intelligence goes rogue, gives us a chance to make sure that when this happens, the reporting occurs and occurs immediately," Attorney-General Burke said alongside the PM. SBS quotes Albanese's frame: "our task is to make sure that we shape AI rather than AI shape us." Nine flags the regulator model as a world-first in legislated AI penalty enforcement; DW notes it aligns with the Joint Select Committee on AI's interim report.

Fireworks AI's second breach: LLMJacking crew reaches dev machines through a third-party cloud, exposing billing data and partial model weights

SecurityInfrastructure

AI-infrastructure startup Fireworks AI disclosed Monday its second security incident in weeks. Its updated bulletin says attackers breached an unnamed third-party cloud provider hosting Fireworks' R&D machines on October 23, used that access on October 24 to reach Fireworks development systems and run an unauthorized workload, and The Hacker News reports the fallout now includes account and billing information — billing/shipping addresses, invoice and payment histories, account balances, credit usage — plus secrets, datasets and subsets of incomplete model weights for a limited set of accounts. Payment card numbers are not stored and were unaffected; the company is working forensically with Palo Alto's Unit 42 and notifying affected customers.

Bloomberg notes Fireworks serves over 10,000 customers including Notion, Cursor and Harvey, and a person familiar put the exposed file count in the tens of thousands. The motive, per The Register, is "LLMJacking" — reselling unauthorized access to frontier-model inference through illicit routing gateways; CEO Lin Qiao posted that "attackers are not after weights — most downloads were partial and stopped." Forbes frames the systemic risk: enterprises rushing to build cheaper bespoke models ship proprietary data to exactly these platforms, making one compromise a multi-customer event — a Render founder shared a Notion warning that some users' chats with Notion's AI assistant may have been exposed. The bulletin links Google Threat Intelligence's November 2 write-up of the LLMJacking economy as context.
