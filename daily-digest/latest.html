The day the primes fell: Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis "working with Claude" — 17 million lines of Lean, independently compiled by MIT's Andrew Sutherland ("the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero"), while the Clay Institute still lists the 167-year-old problem as unsolved

TOP STORYResearchModels

One day after Anthropic's stock market debut, Alpöge posted a four-page note from his personal website: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis" — and, per the note, the method "easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too," The Associated Press and The Verge (both Nov 20) report. The hypothesis, posed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, claims the zeros of the zeta function that encode prime-number distribution all sit on a single vertical line; it is one of seven Clay Millennium Prize problems, carries a $1M bounty, and Nature (Nov 20) notes the announcement lands in Riemann's bicentenary year. Only the formalization was released: roughly 17 million lines of Lean code — a textual write-up Alpöge estimates at more than 250,000 pages — with the author saying he has "not yet mastered" all of the mathematics involved and that he worked with Claude over about six weeks (BBC, Nov 20). "Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line," Alpöge wrote, per AP.

The verification picture by Saturday morning is unusually strong for a claim this large — with real caveats. Scientific American (Nov 20) reports that independent researchers, including MIT number theorist Andrew Sutherland, compiled Alpöge's Lean result separately and re-checked it with the Lean kernel using only the three standard axioms: "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero," Sutherland said — and in SA's first report he called it "an historic moment… the world has changed… perhaps the single most important step in the proof was building a machine that can think." But what hasn't finished: the "comparator run" that checks the code doesn't quietly redefine the theorem's statement and compiles under several independent kernels, explained by Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College; Terence Tao told BBC verification "is still some way off" and the digestion process will take time; and AFP (Nov 20) and The Verge both note Clay's rules require journal publication plus two years of general acceptance before the prize — the problem remained listed as open on Clay's site hours after the announcement. Oxford's James Maynard told Nature: "If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone"; Montreal's Andrew Granville was harsher, calling the released document "garbled" and saying the write-up should have come first.

The timing was the story behind the story. Alpöge said he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei "late Sunday night" — before the Nov 19 IPO — asking about his ability to announce, and wrote: "There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false" (BBC, AP, Nature, which ties Granville's skepticism to the stock's first-day fade). Anthropic itself declined to comment to AP, Nature and AFP, and had said back in August that an unreleased Claude had improved a long-standing related result while warning "we don't expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis" (The Verge). Riemann specialist Brian Conrey told AFP: "It's a monumental moment in human history… I really did not see this coming at all," while adding he believes "there will be simpler ways to do it." SA's original piece also credits October's OpenAI math-results dump with the "first chink in the problem's armor" — the lab war has now formally entered the millennium-prize tier.
