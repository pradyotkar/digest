The "fast and cheap" model that isn't cheap: Gemini 4 Hydrogen outscores frontier Argon on the Artificial Analysis Index at 51 — for exactly what Flash is scheduled to cost on January 1

Model ReleasePricing

Google announced Gemini 4 Hydrogen on November 12 (updated Nov 13) as the execution model of the Gemini 4 family — "built for rapid execution across diverse agentic workflows" while Carbon and Argon handle deep reasoning — at a flat $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output across its entire 1M-token context window, with $0.075/M cached input, four thinking levels (low/medium/high/xhigh), Batch/Flex at half price and Priority at 1.8x, and — unlike Carbon — a spot on the API free tier. The scorecard: 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at default high (54 at xhigh), versus Argon's 50 and Flash's 31, with Google claiming tasks complete ~2.5x faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash. But VentureBeat's breakdown lands the punchline: Hydrogen costs exactly what Flash goes to from January 1, 2027 ($1.50/$7.50, up from the $0.75/$3.75 intro rate) — so the budget tier's discount is ending and a better model has been bolted to the new number, with no launch promotion and the old thinking_budget knob gone. VentureBeat also notes Hydrogen runs ~15x the token price of Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Luna and Meta's Muse Ember ($0.10/$0.50 class), though on Terminal-Bench 4.0 Hydrogen posted 54% versus 40.9% for Ember and 12.6% for Luna. SiliconANGLE counts the cadence: three Gemini 4 models in just over six weeks.

Google's answer to the price question is orchestration: Antigravity's new Orchestrator switches between Carbon and Hydrogen mid-session, reaching near-Carbon task success on Terminal-Bench while consuming 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster. That split — frontier model for architecture, Hydrogen for writing/testing/verifying — is the pitch; the practical advice for buyers is to compare verified jobs completed, not token prices.

AI attacks AI in the new security product wars: OpenAI ships Daybreak Red Team and Defense Factory — agent fleets that pen-test your apps and draft the patches — while GPT-6 Cyber got root on Linux 948 times in research

AI AgentsProductSecurity

At its "Cyber at the Frontier" event on November 12, SecurityWeek reports OpenAI introduced two Daybreak products: Daybreak Red Team, where multiple agents use source code and app context to hunt attack paths and confirm whether reported flaws — from code scanners, bug bounties or disclosure programs — are actually exploitable, running in isolated environments with Guardian review enforcing scope boundaries; and Daybreak Defense Factory, which traces vulnerabilities through the codebase and prepares patches and tests. The loop stays human: WebProNews details that Defense Factory ingests findings from Wiz, Snyk and Endor Labs, watches connected GitHub repos and new commits, lets engineers adjust patches with Codex, and opens draft pull requests — nothing ships on its own. Nancy Wang, who leads cyber-applied AI, framed Red Team to The Register as "superhuman pen-test at the scale of automated continuous scanning" — and The Register's headline noted the sales pitch: orgs that don't use such tools are doomed. Access: Red Team via application for early access, Defense Factory as a ChatGPT preview plugin for Daybreak workspaces; no pricing announced.

The engine is the gated GPT-6 Cyber. SecurityWeek's separate story runs the numbers: 48.5% on ExploitGym (vs 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber), a Critical cybersecurity rating under OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, and in kernel research it reproduced 1,724 Linux vulnerabilities and built functional privilege-escalation exploits for 948 — against 166 for GPT-5.6 Sol with the same tooling. The discipline metric OpenAI emphasizes: it refrained from exploiting deliberately-easy-but-off-limits decoy services in 99.93% of tests without deployment prompts, 100% with them. Access remains limited to approved Daybreak Red customers, with a sanctioned-testing group for critical-infrastructure defenders.

The agent control plane comes home — with strings: Anthropic self-hosts Claude Managed Agents in your cloud or data center, but the license keeps the brain in the cloud and the clock running

AI AgentsEnterprise

Anthropic announced on November 12 that self-hosted Claude Managed Agents is in private beta: companies run the whole agent service — harness, sandboxes, credentials and session history — in their own cloud or on-prem, while each model request goes to the provider you choose (Anthropic, Amazon Bedrock, Google's Agent Platform, Microsoft Foundry, or your own gateway). Anthropic says it does not remotely access your servers or their logs; access is via account team, tbreak notes there's no published price, nothing migrates from the hosted service, and webhooks and scheduled runs are missing; a persistent deployment wants PostgreSQL 16 or later. The docs draw the new map precisely: cloud sandboxes (Anthropic hosts everything), self-hosted sandboxes (you host execution only), or full self-host (you host service + sandboxes; the model stays wherever you point it).

The New Stack read the license, and the caveats are the story: Anthropic ships the server as a proprietary binary you can't modify or patch yourself, the gateway can only route to Claude models, and — most pointedly — customers have eight hours to act when Anthropic flags a safety risk in the software. The pattern for the enterprise-agent era, in one product: the body runs in your data center; the brain stays metered, upstream, and on Anthropic's terms. It's a widening menu the same week The Artificial Brief flagged Thinking Machines releasing Inkling 2 Small's weights and Huawei open-sourcing MACaQ, a quantization method claiming a 122B model taken to 4-bit in 64 minutes on 32.5GB of GPU memory — downloaded weights that, the Brief cautions, transfer the infrastructure and security obligations to you instead.
