Microsoft shows its own stack: MAI revealed "Rocket," an in-house reinforcement-learning framework supporting weeks-to-months training runs across thousands of GPUs, currently post-training the three-trillion-parameter MAI Base 2 from scratch — no third-party distillation — with a model that already passed Claude Opus 4.8 on an aggregate reasoning benchmark and is "on track" to overtake GPT-5.6 Sol within weeks

MODEL RELEASEAI Economics

Reported by ANI (today, Nov 24), Rocket exists because long RL runs compound tiny failures: "over runs that long, small numerical mismatches and system inefficiencies compound," so Microsoft built checkpointing and resource-recovery that lets training continue after hardware failures instead of restarting. The stated purpose is reducing dependence on other companies' models — MAI Base 2 is being improved from the base stage through reinforcement learning rather than distillation. Nadella's framing is pure cost curve: "Outcome per dollar is the frontier. We're advancing it at every layer: FLOPs-efficient pretraining (MAI Base 2), long RL climbs (Rocket), and token-efficient models (seven new MAI models announced last week)." Rocket has already driven progress on an aggregate benchmark combining public and private evaluations, beating Claude Opus 4.8 with GPT-5.6 Sol as the named next target. The distribution strategy is already visible in the rate cards: MAI Code 2 and MAI Code 2 Flash landed in GitHub Copilot on Nov 17 at promotional credit pricing of $0.50/$2.50 and $0.05/$0.25 per million tokens through May 2027 (ToolColumn's dated changelog, citing github.blog) — cheap first-party models riding the world's largest coding-assistant install base. Context from the same week's boards: Nadella conceded last week that Claude Code "caught Microsoft off guard" — Rocket is the structural answer, building the model layer so the interface layer stops moving under him.

Anthropic ships "enterprise frontier safeguards": a Bloomberg-reported data-protection option that moves the 30-day prompt/response retention it has always required off its own servers and into the customer's cloud — customer-held encryption keys, automated scans every 15 minutes for cyber-attack signals, customer decides how to respond to alerts — rolling out first on Mythos and Fable

EnterprisePrivacy

ANews, summarizing Bloomberg (today, Nov 24): the early version of "enterprise frontier safeguards" is a genuine shift from Anthropic's prior policy requiring customer data to sit on its own servers for 30 days. The nuance matters — the 30-day retention doesn't go away; the location and the keys do. Customers still keep prompts and responses for 30 days to enable detection of sophisticated cyberattacks, but store them in their own cloud environments, hold the encryption keys themselves (per safeguards head Jon Turow, via Bloomberg), and receive automated alerts — with Anthropic software scanning every 15 minutes — to decide themselves how to respond. The design is a direct answer to yesterday's and today's safety stories: if frontier models need monitoring that requires retained transcripts, and enterprises won't hand transcripts to a lab, then the lab ships the monitor and lets the customer keep the data. Timeline consistency is good — Bloomberg reported the work in August, Anthropic said in September it would arrive "in the fall," and it landed in the fall, six days after the company's record Nasdaq listing made enterprise trust the thing its $1.5-trillion-ish valuation is priced on. The obvious adjacency: this is the deployment tier story 2 predicts for "too powerful to ship" models — limited-access, enterprise-shaped, monitored — and it pairs with the "Know Your Agent" governance rails Singapore's MAS floated on Friday. Watch: whether the same architecture is offered to OpenAI-style lab-internal models, whether any regulated-industry customer publishes a real deployment, and whether competitors' compliance teams treat "15-minute scans" as a new bar.

The anti-agent wall goes up: Signal will block screenshots in its iOS app and switch on screen security the moment an Android AI assistant tries to read the screen, calling on Apple, Google and Microsoft to ship per-app agent kill-switches — joining United Airlines, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Charles Schwab, which have already restricted or blocked third-party AI agents

AI AgentsPrivacy

CNBC (today, Nov 24) reports the privacy app's core complaint is that OS-level agents with screen awareness — Google's Gemini, Apple's Siri — can "scan" anything on a user's screen and capture Signal data outside Signal's control. "It is a serious problem that Signal — and application developers beyond — lack robust tools to prevent AI agents from undermining application-level privacy," said Signal Foundation president Meredith Whittaker in the app's Friday blog post. Signal also flagged third-party agents such as Meta's Muse as a risk — they can quietly reach private app content through permissions granted during setup — and demanded Apple, Google and Microsoft ship simple technical tools and documentation so developers can deny agents access to sensitive app data. This isn't an isolated stand: the same story catalogues United Airlines restricting unauthorized agents from booking or managing tickets (Nov 11) over data-exposure and aviation-rule risks; Jamie Dimon calling outside agents "too risky" for JPMorgan clients (October); Morgan Stanley blocking third-party agents from its platforms; Charles Schwab CEO Richard Wurster saying the firm blocks unauthorized agents outright; and Singapore's OCBC refusing to let third-party agents move money autonomously. The counter-example is instructive: Singapore Airlines chose to be an early partner of Meta's Muse instead — the agent economy is splitting into rails that open to agents (with mandated KYA protocols, story 4 yesterday) and walls that close (with screenshot blocks). Watch: whether Apple/Google expose per-app agent opt-outs at the OS level, whether Signal's iOS screenshot block spreads to other privacy-critical apps, and whether agentic-commerce merchants and banks start treating "agent allowed" as an explicit, auditable permission.

GLM-5.4's mechanics, in Z.ai's own words: 28 layers of the GLM-5 base run twice per forward pass, a design the lab says lets ~750 billion parameters match 2–3 trillion-parameter-class intelligence — 10–14% shorter reasoning traces for ~10% more training compute — with no published benchmarks yet, API and weights still gated on safety review, and early users reporting marathon-thinking runs

MODEL RELEASEOpen Models

Yesterday's launch got its technical explainer today. The Specialty News (Nov 24) reports Z.ai validated the looped design on base models of 1B, 4B, 30B and 750B parameters before shipping; in a same-parameter-count comparison at 30B, the looped version used about 10% more training compute while average reasoning answers got 10–14% shorter on tests like GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro — evidence, the lab argues, that more of the thinking happens inside the loop instead of in written-out steps. Z.ai is candid about the open questions: it says looped models trained from scratch still trail standard ones at equal compute — which is why GLM-5.4 loops a strong existing base — and it has not yet published GLM-5.4's own benchmark scores, with early Coding Plan users flagging long marathon-thinking runs on some tasks; throughput is quoted at up to 200 tokens/second off-peak and 70–80 at peak. BenchLM's release tracker (retrieved Nov 24) counts GLM-5.4 as the only confirmed Nov 23–24 release, alongside this week's Gemini 4 Neon (Nov 18, $3/$15 per million tokens, paid tier only) and Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini. The parameter ambiguity from yesterday persists — the "750B" figure now comes with the explanation that under Loop Transformers, parameter count and capability are decoupled, which is precisely why the claim "750B ≈ 2–3T class" is the most interesting open-model number this side of the DeepSeek round news. Watch: the promised benchmarks (and whether third-party evals reproduce the equivalence), whether the API/weights un-gating clears the lab's own safety review, and whether any Western lab adopts looped layers — the architecture is the story, not the parameter sheet.
