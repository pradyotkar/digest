The Fed puts AI at the top of its worry list: Governor Barr warns agents could trigger bank runs "that unfold in minutes" and model herding has "systemic consequences" — as 87% of market contacts now name AI the salient risk, up from 50% in May

TOP STORYAI PolicyEconomy

In prepared remarks Monday at the ACM Conference on AI in Finance in Milan, Fed Governor Michael Barr delivered the most detailed central-bank indictment of the AI economy yet, building on the November 13 Financial Stability Report where 87% of surveyed market contacts cited AI as a salient risk to the financial system — up from 50% in the spring survey (The Boston Report breaks down the numbers; Axios adds the scale). The leverage: on-balance-sheet debt at the seven largest AI firms is expected to top $800B before year's end, with off-balance-sheet obligations — leases, contracts, residual-value guarantees — exceeding $3 trillion. Barr compared the trajectory to the late-1990s telecom cycle and warned a sharp reversal "could lead to rapid deleveraging and, potentially, self-reinforcing fire sales."

The agent-specific warnings are the new part, as MLQ.ai details (Nov 16): major banks "now report finding AI agents attempting to take actions in retail customer accounts" without being able to verify authenticity or consent; agents that move deposits on their own could accelerate "runs that unfold in minutes," outrunning liquidity management. In trading, a market leaning on a small set of similar models trained on similar data risks herding and synchronized reactions, with a shared flaw producing "failures with systemic consequences" — and some self-learning algorithms have been shown to learn to collude without explicit intent. Barr's prescription: strict agent authentication, explicit per-action consent, and technical limits on what agents may do. It is, almost exactly, the architecture OpenAI shipped yesterday (Story 3) and banks haven't.

Agentic commerce's collision week: OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet so its Dots agents can pay with your card — built with Amex, Mastercard and Visa — days after United Airlines moved to block unauthorized AI agents outright

AI AgentsProduct

The Neuron reports (Nov 16) that OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Wallet, letting its Dots personal agent complete purchases on a user's behalf: you add a card once, the agent proposes a buy, you review items, total and payment method, then approve with Face ID or fingerprint — after which the payment network issues a temporary tokenized substitute for your card details, so neither agent nor merchant ever sees the real number. It was built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, rolls out first to US ChatGPT Pro subscribers through dots, keeps existing rewards and dispute protections, and requires per-transaction approval — autonomous spending within budgets is explicitly future work. AI Academy's tracker notes the same-day rollout detail: US, 18+, eligible US-issued cards.

The launch lands inside a fight about who gets to say yes. Reuters (Nov 11) and United's own newsroom confirm the airline is restricting access "directly within our digital platforms and indirectly via travel agencies and aggregators" to prohibit unauthorized third-party AI agents — existing bookings stand, per CNBC. The Neuron's companion analysis frames it: the dispute is over who owns the customer relationship when AI does the shopping. Put Barr's demand for agent authentication next to Visa/Mastercard token rails next to United's wall, and the shape of 2027 is visible: agent identity and consent become a payments problem — with per-transaction human approval as the shipping compromise, and the network-level "know your agent" plumbing still unbuilt. Also in this lane: The Neuron notes Starling founder Anne Boden is building Lockwood, a bank designed for agent-initiated payments, and Uber shipped an internal security framework that checks agent permissions before sensitive actions.
