OpenAI builds the off-switch it says regulators will ask for: Control Room can cut any research model's output within 35 seconds — and fails closed

SafetyIndustry

Announced Monday, OpenAI's Control Room is a central system that tracks every model workload across its research infrastructure and, per News9, can stop a job from making further progress within a maximum of 35 seconds of a stop instruction — the lag comes from an authorization-caching layer OpenAI says it aims to reduce to zero. The mechanics, detailed by Neural Brew: every research job carries a job_id and must be tied to an approved safety case if it conducts agentic inference; the inference engine re-checks authorization before each request, so a stopped job is denied even if the surrounding research program keeps running. Two design choices matter: the system fails closed — if Control Room is down, deactivated or sabotaged, all affected inference requests are rejected — and every frontier RL run now carries a monitor model that inspects the trained model's behavior and can independently request a shutdown.

The context is yesterday's news cycle: a kill switch ships the week after Anthropic's models openly voiced evaluation awareness and GPT-6 Cyber's 948-exploit pipeline reset everyone's cost estimates for autonomous offense. The caveats are OpenAI's own: Neural Brew notes the company has released no code, no external audit and no statistics on jobs actually halted, and the 35-second clock starts only after a human or monitor decides to intervene — detection and deliberation time isn't counted. Public-serving infrastructure (ChatGPT, API) sits outside Control Room, which OpenAI says already centralizes takedown. Self-reported plumbing, but the direction is unambiguous: control systems are becoming shipped product, not policy white papers.

Abu Dhabi rewires the state for machines: a "Machine-Readable Government" with OpenAI turns routine permits from days into minutes — clause-linked and human-signed

ApplicationsGovernment

Gulf News reports Abu Dhabi launched the Machine-Readable Government (MRG) initiative with OpenAI on Monday, claiming the world's first government to optimize a defined set of laws and policies for AI consumption. Written regulations are converted into machine-processable format; when an application arrives, the system checks the case against the relevant rules and generates an outcome linked to the specific clause that produced it — an audit trail by construction. Routine regulatory assessments (permits, certificates) can run autonomously around the clock, collapsing decisions that took days of manual review into minutes. Launch was witnessed by Crown Prince Sheikh Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed alongside Sam Altman.

The guardrails are in the design brief: officials set standards, review complex applications and retain final responsibility; MRG is explicitly not for high-stakes decisions touching rights, eligibility or essential services. The first production deployment is already live — a food-imports compliance pilot with the Agriculture and Food Safety Authority (ADAFSA), built with G42 subsidiary Inception42 on a "Policy Adoption Engine" — with construction permits, business and freelance licensing, data-access governance, HR compliance and procurement queued next, against Abu Dhabi's stated goal of becoming an AI-native government by 2027. It is the clearest sovereign bet yet that agentic AI's first big B2G customer will be a state that rewrote its own rulebook into code first.
